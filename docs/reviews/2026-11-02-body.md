# Simulated PC review: body-only draft of "Computing What Adversarial Edits Do to Features" (2026-11-02)

**Executive summary**
1. Scores, assuming competent empty sections: **A (formal methods) Weak reject; B (adversarial ML for malware) Weak reject; C (systems security) Weak accept.** Likely outcome: reject with encouragement. The paper becomes borderline-accept once the top risks are fixed.
2. Top risk 1: **the detector evaluation reads as tautological.** The monotone rows' 0% follows from the theorem. The adaptive attack uses 2 of the 12 operations and evades the *undefended* model on only 8% of files, while the toolkit evades it on 53%. The hand table it is compared against was written by the authors.
3. Top risk 2: **the guarantee rests on two unchecked conditions.** The population must be closed under the catalogue, and the envelopes are unions over a 2,000-file corpus rather than over the population. Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 need both. The formalization records both checks (P1, P2) as not run, and the body never mentions either.
4. Top risk 3: **the evidence for generality and for the derivation's value is thin.** One instance. Four of Table 6's seven hand-rule errors occur only on layouts the population excludes, and inside the population the derived and hand tables give near-identical detectors. The opening also misreads Pierazzi et al.'s $\boldsymbol\eta$, which a Pierazzi-aware reviewer will catch.
5. **The single most important fix:** make the detector evaluation non-circular. Run the closure and stability checks (minutes). Extend the adaptive attack to the full catalogue, so it beats the toolkit on the unconstrained model. Make the out-of-model toolkit outputs, not the 0.0, the headline evidence. Restore the "still detected after every attack" column.

## 1. Three simulated PC reviews

### Reviewer A: formal methods and programming languages

**Summary (own words).** Many feature extractors are folds over pieces of the input: they enumerate "items" (sections, strings, packets), add each item's contribution into commutative-monoid accumulators, and then apply a per-feature formula. The authors make the extractor log the items. They show that, if an edit's effect on the items can be localized (no hidden locating positions; bounded damage regions for scanned items), the new feature vector follows from the old sums plus the delta. This is the standard delta rule for SUM aggregates, with MAX needing the full multiset. They then lift item-level change signs through a sign domain and per-readout monotonicity rules. The result is a sound "may increase / may decrease" table per feature for a catalogue of edit operations, which feeds Gimenez et al.'s monotone-classifier robustness argument. The summary was easy to write. That is a strength: the model is crisp.

**Strengths.**
- The decomposition (items → sums/pools → readouts) is the right abstraction. It separates the format-dependent part (R3/R4, damage regions) from the format-free algebra, and the paper says so explicitly (`_d_framework.tex:204-205`).
- It is honest about what is classical: Gray et al.'s distributive and algebraic aggregates (`_d:76-77`), Wagner and Graham's damage regions (`_d:199-200`), the sign domain (`_d:248`), and Gimenez et al.'s Proposition 1 (`_j:84-85`).
- The pool counterexample (`_j:24-25`) and the traffic longest-gap example (`_d:228-231`) show exactly where an aggregate-only view fails. That justifies keeping the item table.

**Weaknesses (ranked).**
1. **The guarantee's preconditions are not discharged on the instance** (`_d:262-268`, `_d:286`, `_e:108-112`). Theorem 2 needs envelopes that contain the true ones over $F$. For operations stated in elements, the EMBER envelopes are unions of per-file signatures over a 2,000-file corpus (`_d:251-254`; verified: `docs/formalization.md` §6.2 and the "population caveat" in §9). A file in the population with a layout the corpus lacks can exceed them; the formalization itself documents a case (four `.bss` files whose raw data is the header). Corollary 1 also needs $F$ closed under $\mathcal{M}$, and closure is checked only on original files (verified: `docs/formalization.md` §9, "closure caveat"; P1 and P2 "not run"). The body states the robustness theorem and then calls the detectors ones "no in-model edit can evade" (`_f:140`) without either condition. This is a correctness gap in how the theorem is applied, not in the theorem.
2. **The formal novelty is modest, and the paper does not say which part is new.** (ii) is the IVM delta rule for SUM; (iii) is the textbook MIN/MAX deletion problem; Theorem 2 is one step of sign-domain abstract interpretation; Corollary 1 is Gimenez et al.'s Proposition 1. What is new is the *modeling*: items with locating positions and margins, the term vocabulary with its sign table, and the readout rules. The paper should claim exactly that. Today, "Theorem" labels on a few lines of algebra invite the "known technique renamed" verdict. Provenance semirings (Green et al., already in `main.bib` as `green2007provenance`), self-adjusting computation (Acar), and higher-order IVM are not mentioned, and the sign domain is uncited (`_d:248`).
3. **The requirements carry the content, and they are validated, not proved** (`_d:185-203`, `_e:30-35`). R3 is "the record of locating positions is complete". On EMBER it was completed by single-byte flips after a first round missed three parser rules (`_e:30-32`). Single-byte flips do not exercise positions that matter only jointly. The guarantee is therefore conditional on an empirical completeness test. That is acceptable if stated as such; the paper states it as unconditional.
4. **A wrong table entry:** "count, keyed … Modified $\{0\}$" (`_d:111`, `_j:40`). That holds only if the key is part of the item's identity. If a modification can change an item's key (an import whose library name is rewritten; a Drebin call site whose API changes), the old key's count falls and the new key's rises. The signed-hashed row handles exactly this case ("in the old bucket and the new"). No EMBER operation in the catalogue triggers it, so no result changes. But it is a soundness error in the general vocabulary, which is the part claimed as format-free. (Verified against `docs/formalization.md` Lemma 3, which has the same entry; this is a factual correction, allowed under the freeze.)
5. **The proofs refer to parts of (R4) the body never numbers** (`_j:12-14` cites "parts (i) and (ii) of (R4)" and "part iii"; `_d:196-200` states R4 as one sentence). A careful reader cannot check part (i) of Theorem 1 as written.

**Questions.**
1. What is $F$ for EMBER, exactly: a decidable predicate (`plain_layout`), or "the files on which the corpus envelopes hold"? How do you know the two coincide?
2. Is the plain-layout population closed under each of the twelve operations? In particular, can an overwrite of loader-ignored header fields or a gap insertion make a plain file non-plain, which would hand the attacker the unconstrained fallback?
3. Why not obtain Theorem 1 automatically by running the unmodified extractor under self-adjusting computation or dynamic dependency tracking? What does the item abstraction buy beyond Theorem 2?

**Score:** Weak reject (confidence: high). **One change up a level:** state Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 with the population and closure conditions discharged on the instance (P1, P2, and the precondition re-checked on every edited file), correct the keyed-count entry, and add a paragraph that names precisely what is new against IVM, provenance semirings, and abstract interpretation.

### Reviewer B: adversarial ML for malware detection

**Summary (own words).** The authors instrument the EMBER 2024 extractor so it logs which file regions feed which accumulators. From that log they compute the side effects of PE edits, and per-feature monotonicity directions for a catalogue of twelve edit operations taken from secml-malware-style attacks. Dropping two-way features and training a monotone LightGBM on the rest gives a detector with 87-90% TPR at 1% FPR that the toolkit attacks and the authors' own adaptive search never evade on "plain-layout" files. Gimenez et al.'s attack-derived PV feature selection keeps far fewer features (56% TPR) and falls to the adaptive search on 16 of 22 files. Summary is easy to write; the pitch is clear.

**Strengths.**
- A monotone EMBER detector at 89.6% TPR at 1% FPR (`_f:164`), against 29.1% for Gimenez et al.'s 40 expert features (`_f:162`), is a big accuracy jump for a certified-by-design detector.
- Re-extraction from disk for every reported score (`_f:20-21`), and attacks written with byte-level code that does exactly the declared edit (`_e:102-106`).
- PV's hole is computed before any attack runs, and then realized (`_f:192-197`). That is a concrete, useful negative result about a recent defense.
- The import-overwrite bug in EMBER 2024 (`_e:46-53`) is a real finding; maintainers and attackers both care.

**Weaknesses (ranked).**
1. **The adaptive attack is too weak to be the adaptive column.** It uses two of the twelve operations (append and add-section, `_f:30-31`) against a full-catalogue defense. On the unconstrained model it evades 8% (`_f:158`), against 53% for the held-out toolkit attacks. GAMMA import injection alone gets 24/47 (verified: `docs/direction-A-A1-design.md`, Table A2). An "adaptive attacker" weaker than an off-the-shelf black-box attack on an undefended model establishes nothing about a defended one. Its 0.0 on the monotone rows is guaranteed by construction anyway (`_f:177`). This column, as it stands, will be called tautological.
2. **Circular comparison baselines.** The hand direction table (`_f:83-86`) was written by the authors, and its blind spots were shared by their own earlier predictor (`_f:123-124`). A reviewer will read Table 6 as "we fixed our own mistakes". Moreover, inside the population the derived and hand tables give near-identical detectors: the training inputs differ in two features (`entry_name_hash`, `security_size`; verified: `docs/status.md`, 2026-10-28). The derivation's benefit to the detector, on the files the guarantee covers, is therefore small, and the paper does not say so.
3. **The toolkit columns mix in-model and out-of-model edits without saying which is which.** secml-malware's section injection goes through LIEF, which rebuilds the layout. By the paper's own (R5) that is not a catalogue edit (`_e:105-106`). If the toolkit outputs are in the catalogue, 0% is implied by the theorem. If they are not, 0% is *empirical* robustness beyond the theorem, which is the most convincing evidence in the paper, and it is not argued.
4. **The attacks are weak.** Population 10 × 10 iterations (`_f:27`) is a small GAMMA budget. There is no second toolkit (MAB-Malware) and no gradient-free search over the full catalogue. The held-out toolkit set is a split of the same eight attacks (`_f:27-28`).
5. **The routing fallback is the obvious attack surface.** 24% of pilot malware lie outside the population (`_f:180`). An attacker who controls the malware's build can simply make it non-plain and face the unconstrained model. The paper presents the fallback as a coverage statistic, not as a threat.
6. **The expert-set comparison is under the wrong threat model.** Gimenez et al. designed their set for additions. Judging it under the full catalogue (`_f:203-206`) is unfair. The "12 of 40 features can be lowered" is reasoned from their code, not measured (verified: `docs/status.md`, run 106: "Its M_full holes are reasoned, not yet measured"), and it has no adaptive cell (`_f:162`).

**Questions.**
1. Which of the eight toolkit attacks produce files that are catalogue edits under (R5)? Report the in-model and out-of-model outputs separately.
2. Does the adaptive search, extended to rename, slack, header, DOS-stub, and import operations, beat GAMMA on the unconstrained model? What does it do to the expert set and to adversarial training?
3. How many detected malware are evaded by *some* attack, per detector ("still detected" after all attacks)?
4. What fraction of *untruncated* benign and malicious files fail the plain-layout check? The dataset's 4 MB truncation causes most failures (`_e:110-111`).

**Score:** Weak reject (confidence: high). **One change up a level:** an independent, strong attack over the full catalogue (the adaptive search extended to all twelve operations, plus a second toolkit restricted to in-model actions) that beats the toolkit on the unconstrained model, with the toolkit columns split into in-model and out-of-model outputs.

### Reviewer C: generalist systems security

**Summary (own words).** The paper argues that, for common static-feature pipelines, you do not need to run attacks to know how an attacker's edits move the features. You can compute it from a logged extractor, exactly per edit and conservatively per threat model. It shows this on EMBER for Windows executables, finds that a careful hand analysis got several directions wrong, and uses the computed directions to train a malware detector with a robustness guarantee over twelve edit types at about 4 points of TPR. I could write this summary, but I could not say from the body *what a defender does differently tomorrow*. Is the deliverable the detector, the tool, or the method?

**Strengths.**
- A clear, general question (what can edits do to features?) with a clean answer for a well-defined class of extractors.
- The detector result is practically meaningful: sound under a stated catalogue at near-standard accuracy.
- The cross-domain table (`_d:82-99`) makes the agnostic claim concrete.

**Weaknesses (ranked).**
1. **One instance for a claim about a class.** Table 1 maps Drebin and CICFlowMeter onto the model, but nothing is built or measured there. The paper mock's contributions 2 and 3 promise a second instance (`docs/paper-mock.md`); the body has none.
2. **The task enabled is unclear in the body.** Exact side effects (Theorem 1) are, in practice, a faster re-extraction. The paper never says what this enables that re-extraction does not, nor reports timing (`_f:35-78`). The directions table enables the detector, but its rivals (hand table, expert set, PV) are presented as strawmen or weakened.
3. **Threat model scope.** The guarantee excludes code rewriting and packing (`_e:74-75`), which real authors use. That is fine for a defense paper in the adversarial EXEmples line, but the discussion must position it.
4. **Table 6's witnesses are PE parser corner cases on truncated files** (`_f:98-111`). Reviewers outside PE will read this as format minutiae, and the derivation's value as "found pefile edge cases".
5. **The cost argument omits the one-time instrumentation effort** (`_f:208-210`, synthetic). Twenty minutes against 40 CPU-hours ignores the person-weeks needed to instrument the extractor and complete its locating positions by testing.

**Questions.**
1. How much code and effort did instrumenting EMBER take, and what would a second extractor cost?
2. What happens when the catalogue grows: is a new operation one declaration?
3. Is there any security-relevant setting where exact side effects (Theorem 1) beat re-extraction, beyond speed?

**Score:** Weak accept (confidence: medium). **One change up a level:** a second, built instance in another domain, with an exactness row and a derived-versus-published-constraints comparison (constraints written by *other* researchers), plus a sentence-level statement of the deliverable.

### Meta-review

**Agreement.** The model is clean and the EMBER engineering is careful and well validated. The headline detector (sound under a catalogue, about 90% TPR) is attractive. All three reviewers find the evaluation's evidence for the derivation's *value* weaker than its evidence for *correctness*. A and B independently find that the guarantee is stated more strongly than the instance supports: closure and population for A, routing and the adaptive attack for B.

**Disagreement.** C weights the problem statement and the practical detector and would accept with a second instance. A doubts that the theory is new enough to be a contribution. B doubts that the evaluation shows anything not implied by the theorem.

**Likely outcome:** reject with encouragement to resubmit. The fixes are mostly cheap: P1, P2, the in-model/out-of-model split, the "still detected" column, a stronger adaptive attack, and reframing Theorem 1. With those done, A and B move to weak accept and the paper is competitive. The remaining gap is an independent hand baseline, which a second instance with published constraints would close.

### What each empty section must do

**Introduction (§1, ~1,000 words).**
- Establish the gap as a *practitioner's* problem. A defender who wants a monotone or certified detector needs, for every feature, the directions the attacker can move it. Today they get them in one of three ways: by hand (Incer et al., Gimenez et al.'s expert set: few features, low accuracy), by running attacks (PV: misses what the attacks did not try), or by learning (Bostani et al., Simonetto et al.: loose or circular). This answers C's "what task".
- State the deliverable as one sentence: *a detector sound for a stated edit catalogue at near-standard accuracy, derived automatically, and re-derivable when the catalogue changes.*
- Position Theorem 1 as the per-edit foundation that makes Theorem 2's signatures checkable and the adaptive attack cheap, **not** as overturning Pierazzi et al. Answers B and C.
- Must not promise: a guarantee for all files, robustness to packing or code rewriting, breadth beyond what is built, or that Pierazzi et al.'s consolidation problem is solved.

**Key-insight sentence (draft):**
> Most feature extractors in security are folds: they find pieces of the input, add up what each piece contributes, and apply a formula per feature, so once the extractor records its pieces, what an edit does to every feature is arithmetic over the few pieces it touches, and what a whole threat model can do is a sign computation over the pieces it is allowed to touch.

**Background (§2, ~800 words).**
- Pierazzi et al.'s $Z$, $\varphi$, $\mathcal{T}$, $\Gamma$, $\boldsymbol\eta$, *quoted correctly*. Their $\boldsymbol\eta$ arises from what $T$ must do to satisfy $\Gamma$. Split it into (a) the extra problem-space content that consolidation adds, which in this paper is declared per operation, and (b) the extractor's response to the full edit, which this paper computes. This defuses the misreading risk (Reviewer B, and Pierazzi if on the PC).
- Monotone detectors: Incer et al. (hand directions), Gimenez et al. (PV and expert set, Proposition 1), Chen et al. CCS 2021 (hand-specified monotonicity properties, verified training).
- What a defender needs from the reachable set: directions (monotone detectors), regions (verified training), single-edit effects (attack search).
- Must not: re-teach EMBER feature groups at length (format minutiae; Reviewer C).

**Related work (§6, ~700 words).** See §5 below for the list and the fair characterizations.

**Discussion (§7, ~500 words).**
- The routing fallback as an attack surface, stated plainly with the base rate of non-plain layouts on untruncated benign files (answers B). Closure (answers A).
- The requirements are checked on the instance, not proved. Name the checks and what they do not cover (multi-byte locating interactions).
- Scope: packing, recompilation, and code rewriting are whole-file rewrites outside every catalogue, as for Gimenez et al. and Incer et al. One sentence, no packing motivation.
- The cost of instrumenting: lines of code, the rounds of the flip test, what a second extractor took (answers C).
- What does not fit: holistic aggregates, global program analyses, learned representations, time-decayed statistics (already planned).
- Must not: a Threats to Validity section; apologetic tone.

**Abstract.** Problem (directions are written by hand or learned from attacks) → idea (sum-then-readout extractors; record the items) → two results (exact per edit, sound per catalogue) → evidence (EMBER: N features modeled, every hand-rule disagreement the hand rule's error, a monotone detector at X% TPR unmoved by any in-catalogue edit, where attack-derived selection falls to an adaptive attack). Must not mention breadth that is not built.

## 8. Drafts

### (1) Contributions list (for `_b_introduction.tex`; matches what the body supports, with no second instance)

> - A model of the feature extractors security classifiers use, in which an input's pieces (items) add contributions to running totals (sums) that per-feature formulas (readouts) read. It comes with five requirements under which an edit's effect on every feature can be computed exactly from a record the extractor writes while it runs, and a threat model's effect can be bounded soundly as the direction each feature can move. The algebra is classical; what is new is the item model that maps raw-input edits to item changes, and the vocabularies that make directions derivable for every feature.
> - An instrumented EMBER extractor that records 2,480 of its 2,568 features bit for bit, with its requirements checked by single-byte flips, random writes, and re-extraction of every operation of a twelve-operation edit catalogue. Instrumenting it found a bug in EMBER 2024 that lets an attacker erase a file's imports.
> - Derived direction tables that disagree with a careful hand analysis only where the hand analysis is wrong, each disagreement witnessed by a real file and edit, and that, unlike constraints learned from attacks, include the edits no attack happened to try.
> - Monotone detectors over the derived directions that keep 87-90% detection at 1% false positives and that no edit in the catalogue can evade, compared with 29% for a hand-picked monotone feature set. Attack-derived feature selection, in contrast, has a hole the derivation computes in advance and an adaptive attack exploits.

### (2) Key-insight sentence and the introduction's argument

**Key insight.**
> Most feature extractors in security are folds: they find pieces of the input, add up what each piece contributes, and apply a formula per feature, so once the extractor records its pieces, what an edit does to every feature is arithmetic over the few pieces it touches, and what a whole threat model can do is a sign computation over the pieces it is allowed to touch.

**Plan of the argument (one paragraph).** Open with a defender who wants a malware detector that edits cannot evade. Monotone detectors give that, provided someone states, for every feature, which way the attacker can move it. Today that statement comes from hand analysis (sound only for the few features an expert can reason about: 40 features, 29% detection), from running attacks (Gimenez et al.'s PV: sound only for the attacks that were run), or from learning (loose, or circular). Pierazzi et al. explain why: the feature mapping is treated as a black box, so the consequences of an edit can only be observed. Then the insight: for the large class of extractors that are folds, the box can be opened cheaply, and the consequences computed. State the two results (exact per edit; sound per catalogue) and what they need (five requirements, checked on the instance). State the evidence on EMBER: bit-exact recording; zero wrong predictions; every hand-rule disagreement a hand error; a monotone detector at 89.6% detection that no catalogue edit can evade, including out-of-catalogue toolkit outputs, where PV falls to an adaptive attack on 16 of 22 files. Close with the contributions list and one sentence of scope: the guarantee is per catalogue and per population; whole-file rewrites are outside it.
