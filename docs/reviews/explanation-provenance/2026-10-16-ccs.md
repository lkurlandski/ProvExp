# Pre-submission review: "Explanation Provenance for Malware Detectors" (CCS 2027, Cycle A)

**Executive summary**
1. **Verdict:** as drafted, a *weak reject* at CCS (confidence: fairly confident). The method is clean and the pullback results are solid. The weak point is that both security applications are compared against baselines a reviewer will call too weak.
2. **Risk 1:** the audit, the pushforward's showcase, is run only on contiguous header fields. That is the case where the pushforward reduces to "sum Integrated Gradients over each parsed field" (Proposition 3(iv)). The paper already builds that field map for its own oracle (`_e_pullback.tex:25`), and it never runs it as a baseline.
3. **Risk 2:** the theory is elementary, and no experiment runs under its conditions (GBDT, data-flow features, background baselines). The Influence axiom is also contradicted by the construction. A reviewer will call the Shapley grounding decorative.
4. **Risk 3:** the disagreement triage has no security outcome and no attacker, and its "byte vs. window" claim is tested only against 256 windows, never against field-level resolution. Separately, the blue synthetic results (Android, MalConv retraining) are a desk-reject risk if they are still in the paper at submission.
5. **Fix first:** run the field-level baselines (pushforward vs. per-field IG sum, and the pullback coarsened to fields). They cost hours on existing data. Then rewrite the claims around what the results show the pushforward adds over a parser.

Scope note: the user asked me to focus on contributions and methodology, not on pilot numbers, so I do not re-litigate pilot sample sizes beyond one risk-register line. Throughout, **[V]** marks something I verified in the source or on the web, and **[I]** marks an inference with what would settle it.

## 1. Simulated PC review

**Summary (my own words).** The paper instruments the EMBER v3 feature extractor so that, for each PE file, it emits a sparse nonnegative matrix P. Rows are the file's bytes plus a "null" row, and P[i,j] is byte i's share of feature j. For features that are additive over the items they read (fields, counts, histograms, hashed counts), the share is the byte's Shapley value in a game in which the feature is computed from the items present, with the parse held fixed. For entropies and averages, every byte read gets one unit. P gives two linear maps:
- the **pullback** Pa spreads a feature attribution (TreeSHAP on a GBDT) over the bytes;
- the **pushforward** PᵀD⁻¹b splits a byte attribution (Integrated Gradients on MalConv) among the features that read each byte.

For a linear model the pullback equals the pipeline's Shapley values. The pushforward is the first Landweber step of a weighted least-squares inverse of the pullback.

The evaluation reports four findings:
- **Pullback faithfulness:** at matched window resolution the pullback is as faithful as occlusion, at one extraction instead of one per window. At byte resolution it removes more evidence. It recovers a planted 6-byte trigger exactly.
- **Pushforward faithfulness:** its faithfulness is bounded by the resolution of its source. On MalConv it beats a surrogate GBDT.
- **Triage application:** the pullback compares the GBDT and MalConv on single files and labels disagreements as "blind" (coverage gap) or "oppose" (same bytes read in opposite directions).
- **Audit application:** the pushforward aggregates MalConv's IG over 2,000 files into named features. It finds 9 header fields whose loader-safe modification evades MalConv on 12.4% of detected malware. Zeroing those fields before scoring is a partial mitigation.

The summary was easy to write. That is a strength: the contribution is clear.

**Strengths.**
- S1. A clean, general abstraction. P with a null element, the two transfers, and conservation (`_c_method.tex:62-83, 163-214`). It reads as a reusable object, not a one-off hack, and the domain-agnostic framing is credible.
- S2. Instrumentation that is verified and cheap. It adds 616 lines, reproduces the original features exactly, and costs 0.15 s per median file (`_d_setup.tex:46-48`). The black-box estimate independently validates the additive groups (median total variation 0.00, `_e_pullback.tex:58-61`).
- S3. The pullback faithfulness experiment is well controlled. Coarsening the pullback to occlusion's windows separates resolution from information (`_e_pullback.tex:20-22, 33-40`). Ties break by a seeded permutation, and two fills are tested.
- S4. The planted triggers are chosen from data statistics, not from an explainer, which avoids circularity (`_e_pullback.tex:84-86`). Using them as ground truth without deletion is good practice.
- S5. The audit's attack stage is honest. Fresh held-out malware, a matched control drawn from "other loader-ignored fields", CIs, and a mitigation framed as a trade-off rather than a free fix (`_f_pushforward.tex:164-193`).
- S6. Proposition 3(iii) gives a crisp identifiability statement: features with identical provenance cannot be separated. The twins oracle (R 0.86 → 0.95, `_f_pushforward.tex:37-40`) confirms it empirically. Reviewers like a theory that predicts a measured gap.

**Weaknesses (ranked by severity).**
- W1 (major, possibly fatal for the pushforward contribution). **The audit exercises only the regime where the pushforward is trivial.** Candidates are restricted to features whose credited content is "a run of at most 256 bytes" (`_f_pushforward.tex:124-125`), and every confirmed feature is a header field (Table 4). A header byte is read by one field feature and by the byte histogram and entropy windows, whose columns spread over thousands of bytes, so D_ii is dominated by the field. The pushforward therefore reduces, up to a negligible histogram leak, to "sum IG over the field's bytes". That is the region overlay of Coull & Gardner and Bose et al. at field granularity, which Proposition 3(iv) itself names as the special case.
  - The paper dismisses this alternative as needing "great manual effort" (`_f_pushforward.tex:117-119`). Yet Section 5.1 already built exactly this map for the field-occlusion oracle: "uses a PE parser to occlude each header field and section in turn" (`_e_pullback.tex:25-26`) **[V]**.
  - A reviewer will ask why that baseline is absent, and will read "automatically, for any feature extractor" as untested, since only one extractor is evaluated.
- W2 (major). **The theory does not cover any experiment, and part of it is inconsistent.**
  - Proposition 2 needs a linear model, Shapley provenance in every column, and the "no items present" baseline (`_c_method.tex:178-186`). The experiments use a GBDT, data-flow columns for entropy and strings (`_d_setup.tex:39-41`), and background-set explainers. The text concedes this (`_c_method.tex:191-195`).
  - The Influence axiom says P_ij > 0 *iff* z_i influences x_j (`_c_method.tex:71`). Yet pointers and size fields, which do influence features, are assigned zero by convention (`_c_method.tex:132-133`), and the axiom is then silently redefined to "belongs to an item x_j is computed from".
  - The proofs are one-line consequences of linearity and the unanimity-game decomposition. An ML reviewer will see Propositions 1–2 as textbook. A security reviewer will see "Shapley-grounded" in the abstract (`_a_abstract.tex:8`) as framing that the evaluation never uses.
- W3 (major). **The pullback's application has no security outcome, and its key claim lacks the right baseline.**
  - Triage labels (blind/oppose/agree) are validated only by deletion responses on 25 files per cell (`_e_pullback.tex:195-212`). Nothing shows an analyst or a pipeline doing something better with them.
  - The threat model introduces an attacker (`_e_pullback.tex:128-129`) who never appears in an experiment, and the paper defers attacks to future work (`_e_pullback.tex:134-136`, `_h_discussion.tex:36-40` with a "load-bearing" TODO).
  - "A distinction that vanishes over windows of bytes" (`_b_introduction.tex:46`) is tested only at 256 windows (`_e_pullback.tex:222-230`). Since the oppose and agree bytes "interleave within the header", the relevant question is whether **field-level** resolution, which the oracle already has, separates them. If it does, the claim becomes "field resolution", obtainable by parsing at 124 extractions.
- W4 (major). **The headline "byte resolution" gain may be field-level information.**
  - Under Proposition 1 a field is one item and its share is spread *evenly* over its bytes. So the pullback cannot rank bytes within a field except through tiny byte-histogram and entropy terms.
  - Yet the paper explains the 8.0 vs. 4.7 win over the field oracle as "the pullback resolves which bytes *within* a field carry the evidence" (`_e_pullback.tex:50-51`).
  - **[I]** A likelier mechanism: TreeSHAP's interaction-aware ranking of fields beats one-at-a-time field occlusion, plus the pullback's ranking of non-header content.
  - Resolve this by pulling TreeSHAP back to the oracle's field partition (coarsen P to fields) and scoring it. If it matches the byte-level pullback, the 1.7× is an *information* gain at field resolution, not a byte-resolution gain. That would also reshape the abstract's claim.
- W5 (major). **The MalConv pushforward faithfulness test is partly circular, and its baseline is weak.**
  - The pushforward ranking is computed from MalConv's own IG on these bytes. Pulling it back through P and deleting (`_f_pushforward.tex:51-53`) therefore deletes mostly the bytes IG already ranked high. The surrogate never saw MalConv's gradients.
  - "Twice the surrogate" is close to "a gradient-informed summary beats an output imitator". The fair comparisons are other IG-informed summaries into named units: IG summed per parsed field/region (Coull/Bose/Aryal) and IG over windows (already included, 83/100).
- W6 (major). **The parallel structure overpromises.** The pushforward's "A Planted Trigger" (Section 6.2) does not evaluate the pushforward. It uses P to define a support and scores *TreeSHAP and KernelSHAP* against it (`_f_pushforward.tex:79-87`). The parallel test is a trigger that MalConv learns, with the pushforward of MalConv's IG checked against the trigger's features. The intro says the pushforward "also turns the planted trigger into a ground truth for feature-space explainers" (`_b_introduction.tex:48-49`), which is accurate, but the section title and the "three questions" framing (`_f_pushforward.tex:3-5`) imply more.
- W7 (major for the generality claims). **One extractor, one model of each kind.** "For any extractor", "domain-agnostic", and "we expect the same matrix… in other domains" (`_c_method.tex:3-4`, `_b2_background.tex:96`, `_i_conclusion.tex:11-13`) rest on EMBER alone. The Android paragraph that would support them is synthetic (`_h_discussion.tex:57-62`).
- W8 (moderate). **The attack is narrow and the defense is not evaluated adaptively.**
  - The attacker may touch only header fields (`_f_pushforward.tex:105-107`). Published MalConv attacks (padding, DOS-header, section injection; Kolosnjaji et al. 2018, Demetrio et al. 2019/2021) evade at far higher rates, so 12.4% will not impress as an attack.
  - The mitigation is evaluated only against the same field-restricted attacker, plus "other fields" (`_f_pushforward.tex:179-193`).
  - "Removes most of the attack surface we can measure" (`_f_pushforward.tex:191-192`) is true only inside that narrow surface.
- W9 (moderate). **Positioning gaps** (Section 5 below). Aryal et al. 2024 is cited but mischaracterized; it does region-level local *and global* SHAP on MalConv. TRUSTEE (CCS'22) and MAB-Malware's action minimization (AsiaCCS'22) are not cited, and both are defender-side "what does the detector rely on" tools that a CCS reviewer may have written.
- W10 (minor to moderate). **The trust assumption on P is unstated.** P is computed by parsing an attacker-controlled file. Parser differentials between the extractor and the Windows loader could make P point the analyst at the wrong bytes. Explanation manipulation is declared out of scope (`_b2_background.tex:46`), but manipulation of P specifically is not discussed.

**Questions for the authors.**
1. What does the pushforward give, on the audit, that summing MalConv's IG over the fields located by your oracle's PE parser does not? What is the rank correlation between the two over the 354 locatable features?
2. If the GBDT's TreeSHAP is pulled back and coarsened to fields (one cell per parsed field, everything else per byte), how much of the 8.0 vs. 4.7 gap remains?
3. Does disagreement triage at field resolution recover the "oppose" category that vanishes at 256 windows?
4. Since a field's share is spread evenly over its bytes (Proposition 1), what mechanism lets the pullback "resolve which bytes within a field carry the evidence"?
5. Which experiment, if any, satisfies the conditions of Proposition 2? Have you checked the pullback against exact pipeline Shapley values on a linear model?
6. How should a reader reconcile the Influence axiom (iff) with the convention that pointers and sizes get zero share?
7. Can an adversary craft a file so that P, computed by the extractor's parser, misattributes the bytes the loader actually uses?
8. How does the mitigation fare against a known MalConv attack outside the 9 fields, such as overlay padding or DOS-header manipulation?

**Recommendation.** Weak reject (CCS has only Accept / Minor revision / Reject, so in practice Reject). The framework is sound and interesting. But each security application's advantage over the obvious practitioner baseline (a parser-based field map) is not shown, the theory is not used by the evaluation, and the generality claims rest on one extractor. With the field-level baselines run and the claims tightened accordingly, this could move to borderline or weak accept. **Confidence: 3/4** (I know the malware-ML and XAI literature; I cannot run the field-level ablations, which could cut either way).

### 3.2 Drift: implicit adversary assumptions elsewhere

1. `_e_pullback.tex:166-167`: shared bytes are "where a single manipulation weakens both detectors". This assumes an attacker who can *delete* (zero or most-common-value) header bytes, which the comparison's threat model forbids ("program still runs"). The section's caveat at `:134-136` partly covers it; the sentence itself overstates.
2. `_e_pullback.tex:218-219`: oppose bytes are "the place where the missing detector is most easily moved". This is an adversarial or remedial action with no actor in the threat model. Who moves it, and why would a defender want to?
3. `_e_pullback.tex:128-129` introduces an attacker, yet **no experiment in Section 5.3 involves an attacker**, and the cut B9 experiment (attack-caused disagreements) was exactly that test. The threat model is decoration in its current form (see the Section 10 draft for a "setting" alternative).
4. `_f_pushforward.tex:108`: the attacker learns the fields from "an audit like ours". The threat model thus assumes the attacker runs the defender's tool, or gets its output. Then the ethics appendix's argument (`_k_ethics.tex:13-14`) assumes a *query-capable* attacker who "can already search the header for evasive changes directly". These are two different knowledge models; pick one and state it.
5. `_f_pushforward.tex:127-129` (confirmation by scrambling) scrambles fields the loader needs, such as the import address table. That is fine as a reliance measurement, but say it is outside the attacker's capabilities (as for deletion in `_d_setup.tex:97-101`).
6. `_f_pushforward.tex:179-193`: the mitigation's adversary is the same field-restricted attacker. A defense section implies an *adaptive* attacker, but only "other loader-ignored fields" is tested. "Removes most of the attack surface we can measure" is accurate only because the measurable surface was defined narrowly.
7. `_h_discussion.tex:44-46` acknowledges that "an attacker may look for other fields", which is good, but it is not reflected in the threat model's limitations.
8. `_k_ethics.tex:10-11`: "Reliance of MalConv on header content is already documented and publicly exploited". This undercuts novelty of the *finding* and is consistent with framing the audit as *method* rather than *attack*. The intro (`_b_introduction.tex:51-53`) leads with the attack number, though.

### 3.3 Realism

- **Header-field attacker:** realistic and pre-existing (functionality-preserving header manipulations in gym-malware, Anderson et al. 2018; GAMMA/secml-malware, Demetrio et al. 2021; survey cited as `demetrio2021adversarial`). A practitioner would accept it. Cite these to pre-justify the capability rather than relying on "the format's documentation" alone (`_f_pushforward.tex:170-171`).
- **Convenient adversary, in the opposite direction:** the attacker is *weaker* than real MalConv attackers, who can append or pad bytes and inject sections. That makes the mitigation look stronger than it is. A practitioner would not accept "zeroing 12 fields removes most of the attack surface" without a known strong attack in the comparison.
- **Defender with an instrumented EMBER extractor for a raw-byte model:** plausible. EMBER is public and many vendors run feature-based models too. State it as a one-time cost.
- **Trust in the parser (missing):** P is computed by parsing attacker-controlled input with pefile/LIEF-style code. Parser differentials between analysis tools and the Windows loader are a known evasion class (malformed or overlapping headers). One sentence in the threat model, plus an "out of scope" note, closes this.

### 3.4 Defense evaluated against the stated adversary?

Partially. The mitigation is evaluated against the stated field-restricted attacker and a "shift to other fields" variant. **Missing: an adaptive attacker who knows the zeroed fields** and uses any functionality-preserving manipulation, for example an off-the-shelf MalConv attack (DOS-header or padding) re-run on the mitigated model. Without it, keep the mitigation framed as "what the audit tells the defender to weigh" (as `:191` already says) and drop "removes most of the attack surface" from any summary.

## 10. Draft rewrites

### 10.1 Abstract (replaces `_a_abstract.tex:3-16`)

Numbers are the current ones; `[[...]]` marks text that depends on the new analyses in Section 8. About 245 words.

> Static malware detectors come in two kinds: models over engineered features, such as gradient-boosted trees over EMBER, and deep networks over a file's raw bytes. Their explanations cannot be compared or combined. A feature-based detector's explanation names features without saying which bytes they come from; a raw-byte detector's explanation marks bytes that have no name, so it cannot be aggregated across files. We record, by instrumenting the feature extractor once, a *provenance matrix* that gives each byte's share of each feature. For the fields, counts, and hashed counts that extractors are mostly built from, this share is the byte's Shapley value in the feature, recorded at the cost of a single extraction. The matrix transfers explanations in both directions. The *pullback* places a feature attribution on the bytes; the *pushforward* places a byte attribution in named features, giving a raw-byte detector a vocabulary in which its reliance recurs across files. On EMBER-2024 with a gradient-boosted tree and MalConv, the pullback is as faithful as window occlusion at 1/135 of its cost, removes 1.7 times as much evidence at [[byte / parser]] resolution, and recovers a planted six-byte trigger exactly in all 50 test files. We use each direction for a defensive task. Pulled back, the two detectors' explanations meet on the bytes and separate disagreements over the same content from gaps in coverage: when only the tree flags malware, MalConv reads a third of its evidence the other way. Pushed forward over 2,000 files, MalConv's explanations name nine header fields it relies on; changing them only as the Windows loader allows evades 12.4% of detected malware, twice the rate of other loader-ignored fields, and zeroing them before scoring halves this attack.

### 10.2 Contributions list (replaces `_b_introduction.tex:53-67`)

> In summary, we make the following contributions:
> - **Explanation provenance** (Section 3). We define a provenance matrix with a null element for features that no byte produces. We show that, for features additive over the items they read, instrumenting the extractor records exactly each byte's Shapley share. As a consequence, the pullback of a linear model's Shapley values is the exact Shapley value of the whole pipeline over the bytes. The pushforward is the first step of an iteration that recovers everything about a pulled-back explanation that the bytes determine, and nothing else can be recovered. We instrument the EMBER v3 extractor (616 added lines, identical features, 0.15 s per median file).
> - **The pullback is a faithful and cheap problem-space explainer** (Section 5). At equal resolution it matches occlusion at 1/135 of the cost. [[At parser/byte resolution]] it removes 1.7 times as much evidence and beats a parser-based field-occlusion oracle on 88 of 100 files. It recovers a planted structural trigger byte for byte. Applied to a feature-based and a raw-byte detector on the same file, it separates coverage gaps from disagreements over the same bytes, [[a distinction that field- and window-level explanations do not make / with outcome X]].
> - **The pushforward makes a raw-byte detector auditable in named features** (Section 6). Its faithfulness is set by the resolution of the explanation it transfers, not by the transfer. Over 2,000 files it names nine header fields MalConv relies on, confirmed by scrambling, [[and N shared or scattered features that a parsed-field overlay cannot express]]. Loader-safe changes to the nine fields evade 12.4% of detected malware, twice the rate of other loader-ignored fields. Zeroing the audited fields of both methods before scoring cuts the strongest such attack from 16.8% to 2.9% at 3.5 points of detection.

### 10.3 Threat models

**Comparison** (replaces `_e_pullback.tex:127-136`; recommended as a *setting*, since no attacker is instantiated)

> **Setting.** A defender operates a feature-based and a raw-byte detector over the same files, here the GBDT and MalConv, and owns both, so it can explain each. Files on which the detectors disagree, malware caught by only one detector or a benign file flagged by only one, are where an analyst's time goes, because each points to an evasion of one detector, a blind spot, or a false positive. For a single file, the defender wants to know (i) which bytes both detectors rely on (*shared reliance*) and (ii), when they disagree, whether the flagging detector's evidence lies in content the other does not read or in content it reads the other way (*disagreement triage*). We measure both by deleting bytes. Deletion shows where the detectors' evidence lies, not what an attacker can change while keeping the program running; we make no claim about attacks here. We trust the feature extractor: an input crafted to make the extractor parse it differently from the Windows loader could misplace the provenance, and we leave such inputs out of scope.

**Audit** (replaces `_f_pushforward.tex:105-111`)

> **Threat model.** *Defender.* The defender owns MalConv and a labeled corpus, can compute Integrated Gradients, and runs the instrumented extractor of a feature-based detector, which supplies P although MalConv never computes these features. It wants to find, before an attacker does, the content that MalConv's detections rest on and that an attacker can change cheaply. *Attacker.* The attacker wants a malicious file that MalConv detects to be classified benign without changing what the program does. It may change bytes the Windows loader ignores, or set a header field to another value the loader accepts; such header edits are standard in functionality-preserving attacks on PE detectors [gym-malware; GAMMA; Demetrio et al. survey]. It knows which fields MalConv relies on, from an audit like ours or by querying the detector, but needs neither MalConv's parameters nor its explanations. *Limitations.* The attacker does not append or inject content, pack the file, or change code. Such manipulations evade MalConv more easily [Kolosnjaji et al.; Demetrio et al.] and are not what this audit measures: it targets reliance on cheaply changeable content, not MalConv's whole evasion surface. We also do not execute the modified files; which changes the loader accepts follows the format's documentation. *Trust.* The extractor's parse is trusted. A file crafted to make the extractor and the loader parse it differently could misplace P, which we leave to future work.
