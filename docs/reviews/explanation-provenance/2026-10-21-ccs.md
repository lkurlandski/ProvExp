# Simulated CCS Review: "Explanation Provenance for Malware Detectors" (2026-10-21)

**Executive summary**
1. **Scores:** A (XAI) Weak accept; B (malware ML) Weak reject; C (systems security) Weak reject. **Likely outcome:
   Reject.** Under the CCS 2026 rules there is no major revision, and a Cycle-A reject cannot be resubmitted to Cycle B.
2. **Risk 1 (new):** 6.1's "the pullback predicts the GBDT's drop before any edit" has a cheaper exact alternative.
   A defender who knows the attacker's fields and edit rules can apply the edits and re-score at one extraction per
   file. The paper never says why it would predict instead.
3. **Risk 2 (reopened):** the audit's nine confirmed fields are exactly what a PE field overlay finds. The unfiltered
   audit confirmed nothing in shared features, so the pushforward's distinctive mechanism goes unexercised, and "great
   manual effort" will not convince a malware reviewer.
4. **Risk 3 (carried):** one extractor behind "for any extractor". Close behind: the audit and its mitigation leave
   untouched the paper's own strongest attack (DOS stub/slack, 81%), and 6.2 does not say so.
5. **Fix first:** settle Risk 1 with data (rerun the 6.1 edits with new value seeds; 1-2 days) and add the missing
   argument (a defender does not know the attacker's fields; the pullback gives exposure on every field from one
   extraction). Then decide W7 within two weeks, since a second extractor is what answers Risk 2 and Risk 3 together.

Reviewed: `manuscript/main.tex` and every `\input` (abstract through open science, three appendices), the three figure
files and two framework figures under `manuscript/assets/`, Tables 1-4, and `main.pdf` (18 pages; the body runs to

# Pass 1: blind review

## 1. Three simulated PC reviews

### Reviewer A (explainable AI)

**Summary.** The paper instruments a malware feature extractor (EMBER v3) so that it records, for each file, a
nonnegative matrix P of how much each byte contributes to each feature. Through P, a feature attribution is spread onto
bytes (the "pullback", which is G-DeepSHAP's rescale rule), and a byte attribution is split among features (the
"pushforward", a proportional split, with a least-squares "exact" version). For additive features, the recorded shares
equal Shapley values of a per-feature game, so the pullback of a linear model's SHAP values is the pipeline's SHAP
value. The experiments use deletion curves to show that the pulled-back TreeSHAP ranks bytes better than window-based
black-box explainers at far lower cost, and that pushed-forward MalConv attributions beat a surrogate's.

*The summary was easy to write. The method is clear.*

**Strengths.**
1. The problem is real and well posed: a parser is not a model, so chain-rule attribution has no stage-wise matrix
   for it, and the paper builds one by instrumentation (Section 3.3, `_c_method.tex:90-138`).
2. Honest positioning against G-DeepSHAP: the paper states it claims no novelty for the propagation step
   (`_b2_background.tex:129-131`, `_c_method.tex:179-188`).
3. The pushforward's exact form and Proposition 4(iii) give a clean statement of what any transfer into features can
   recover (`_c_method.tex:228-255`), and the 0.80 / 0.86 / 0.95 decomposition on the GBDT (`_c_method.tex:257-262`)
   tests it.
4. The faithfulness evaluation is careful: one byte-deletion scale for every explanation, a verified PE-aware
   operator, both fills, seeded tie-breaking, paired tests, an explicit caveat about out-of-distribution deletion
   (`_d_setup.tex:100-139`).

**Weaknesses (most severe first).**
1. **The theory adds little that is new.** Proposition 2 (`_c_method.tex:111-122`) is the Shapley value of a sum of
   unanimity games, a textbook fact. Proposition 3 is G-DeepSHAP's linear-chain result, which the paper says itself
   (`_c_method.tex:179-181`). The one-step pushforward is a proportional split, and the exact pushforward is weighted
   least squares solved by Landweber iteration (`_c_method.tex:221-246`). All of it is correct, but the paper's
   technical novelty comes down to "instrument the parser." A reviewer will ask whether that is a research
   contribution or an engineering one.
2. **The Shapley grounding does not change the matrix that is used.** For EMBER's additive features, the weight of
   every item is one unit (a field is one item whatever its value; a count adds one per item), so the "Shapley
   provenance" is the same matrix as recording the data flow and spreading it evenly. The other features use the data
   flow by convention. The Shapley framing therefore justifies P after the fact but never yields a different P.
   "The weights in P are not a heuristic" (`_b_introduction.tex:31`) invites the reply that they are the obvious
   heuristic, given a name. *Verified by reading Proposition 2 and Table 1; inferred that no EMBER feature has unequal
   item weights. The paper does not say whether any does. Resolve by checking the instrumented extractor for features
   with non-unit `w_{t,j}`.*
3. **No experiment runs under the exactness conditions, and the baseline mismatch is waved through.** Every
   experiment uses a GBDT with interventional TreeSHAP. Appendix C is, by the authors' own description, "a
   consistency check between two code paths" (`_j3_exact_pullback.tex:16-17`). The step from the proposition to the
   explainers actually used is one sentence: background-set explainers "explain a different change, and the pullback
   splits it in the same proportions" (`_c_method.tex:183-185`). For a count feature whose value is *below* the
   background mean, TreeSHAP gives a negative attribution, and P spreads it over the bytes that are present. The
   bytes are then blamed for what is really an absence. How often does this sign mismatch happen, and how much
   attribution does it carry? *Inferred; not measured in the paper.*
4. **No ablation shows which part of the pushforward does the work.** The paper argues that dividing by D matters
   because a plain sum "would count a byte once for every feature that reads it" (`_c_method.tex:204-206`). It never
   compares the pushforward with that plain sum, Pᵀb, or with a hard assignment of each byte to its highest-share
   feature. The exact pushforward ranks MalConv's features no differently from the one-step version
   (`_e_evaluation.tex:101-102`), which suggests that the split itself matters little on MalConv. If the plain sum also
   ranks alike, the pushforward on MalConv is in practice "sum IG over each feature's bytes."
5. **Faithfulness rests on deletion alone, and the margin at byte level is narrow.** The byte-level pullback beats
   KernelSHAP over 4,096 windows on only 59/100 files (`_e_evaluation.tex:45-47`), with KernelSHAP still rising
   (`_h_discussion.tex:32-36`). "Removes more evidence than any direct explanation" (abstract, `_a_abstract.tex:12-13`)
   depends on the budget. The conclusion's "than any direct explanation we could afford" (`_i_conclusion.tex:7`) is
   the defensible wording.

**Questions for the authors.** (1) Does any EMBER feature have item weights other than one, so that Shapley and data
flow differ? (2) How much TreeSHAP attribution has a sign opposite to Δ_j? (3) How does Pᵀb (no D†) rank MalConv's
features compared with the pushforward? (4) At equal wall-clock time, how does KernelSHAP over windows compare?

**Score: Weak accept** (confidence 3/4). The method is clean and the evaluation careful. The theory is thin, but the
paper does not oversell it much.

**One change to move up a level:** an ablation table for both transfers (Shapley vs. uniform-over-read-bytes P;
pushforward vs. plain sum vs. hard assignment) showing that each design choice changes a measured outcome, or an honest
statement that it does not and why the choice is still right.

### Reviewer B (ML for malware detection)

**Summary.** The authors instrument the EMBER v3 extractor to record which bytes feed each feature, and use the
resulting matrix to move explanations between a LightGBM model and MalConv. Pulled back, the GBDT's TreeSHAP ranks
bytes at least as well as window occlusion, at one extraction. In a defender setting with both models, the pullback
locates bytes both models rely on, predicts how much MalConv-guided header-field edits hurt the GBDT (Spearman 0.48), and
labels disagreements. Pushed forward, MalConv's IG is aggregated across 2,000 files into named EMBER features; nine
header fields are confirmed, loader-safe edits to them evade MalConv on 12.4% of detected malware, and zeroing them at
test time halves that attack at a small cost in detection.

**Strengths.**
1. Uses current, public detectors and data (EMBER-2024, its MalConv checkpoint, a standard LightGBM) and a 1% FPR
   operating point (`_d_setup.tex:56-69`).
2. Loader-safe attack constraints, a held-out set for each step (discovery 2,000, confirmation 191, attack 1,000), and
   controls of equal size (random fields, same-window scrambling) (`_f_applications.tex:224-285`).
3. The "wrong region" attack result is informative: a gradient attack in the DOS stub and slack evades MalConv on
   81% but raises the GBDT's score, and the pullback explains why (`_f_applications.tex:82-89`).
4. Disagreement statistics at scale (6.4% of the test split; `_f_applications.tex:54-56`) motivate the comparison.

**Weaknesses (most severe first).**
1. **For PE, the audit's findings are exactly what a field overlay finds.** All nine confirmed features are header
   fields (Table 4). The candidate filter keeps only features whose bytes form a run of at most 256 bytes
   (`_f_applications.tex:226-227`). Lifting the filter adds no confirmed feature (`_f_applications.tex:265-269`). For a
   4-byte field, the pushforward's score is essentially IG summed over the field's bytes: the field's share of each
   byte dwarfs the byte-histogram's. That is Coull et al.'s and Bose et al.'s overlay, done with `pefile` in a few
   dozen lines. The paper calls this alternative "great manual effort" (`_f_applications.tex:217-219`). A malware-ML
   reviewer will not believe that sentence, and with it goes the main argument for the pushforward over the
   literature in 6.2. The paper's actual differentiator, splitting credit among features that *share* bytes
   (`_b2_background.tex:134-137`), contributes nothing to the confirmed result. *Verified: the filter, the unfiltered
   result, and the fields. Inferred: that the pushforward reduces to a field sum for these fields; resolve with the
   plain-sum ablation in Reviewer A's W4.*
2. **The audit misses the attack surface that matters most, and the mitigation leaves it open.** The same paper shows
   a standard gradient attack in the DOS header, stub, and slack evading MalConv on 81% (`_f_applications.tex:85-86`).
   The audit reports that all confirmed fields lie *outside* the DOS header (`_f_applications.tex:259-262`), and its
   strongest attack evades 16.8%. The zeroing defense (Table 3) does not touch the DOS stub or slack. A reader will
   conclude that the audit finds a second-order weakness and the mitigation leaves the first-order one open. The paper
   explains the reason (an explanation of the original file measures evidence that can be removed, not evidence that
   new content adds; `_h_discussion.tex:44-47`), but only in the discussion, not in 6.2 where the claim is made.
3. **One extractor, one feature model, one raw-byte checkpoint, one dataset, one seed.** The framework claims
   generality ("for any extractor", `_b2_background.tex:99`; "for any feature extractor",
   `_f_applications.tex:219`), and the only evidence beyond EMBER is a blue Android sentence (`_h_discussion.tex:64-69`).
   With the MLP set aside, "the method needs only one explainable model of each kind" (`_f_applications.tex:15-16`)
   rests on one of each.
4. **The pushforward beats the surrogate per file, but barely as an attack.** Pushforward fields evade 12.4% against
   the surrogate's 10.4% (p = 0.027, one seed; `_f_applications.tex:282-283`), and the paper concludes "the stronger
   single method." At scale this could flip. The 2x per-file faithfulness is the robust result. Also, a surrogate is a
   global tool by design; beating it at per-file deletion is expected.
5. **The 6.1 attacker is constructed to fit.** "The attacker explains only MalConv, ... whose gradients and byte-level
   explanations are directly available" (`_f_applications.tex:78-79`). Against a deployed ensemble, neither model's
   gradients are available. The defender red-teaming framing helps, but the asymmetry is asserted, not motivated.

**Questions.** (1) What does `pefile` plus "sum IG over each header field" find on the same 2,000 files? (2) After
zeroing the audited fields, what does the gradient attack of 6.1 achieve? (3) Why exclude the DOS header from the
audit's attack, when the 6.1 attack uses it?

**Score: Weak reject** (confidence 4/4). Solid engineering and a careful protocol, but the applications show what a
PE expert already knows (header reliance), and the generality that would make the automation argument matter is not
shown.

**One change to move up a level:** make the automation argument concrete: a second extractor (any domain) where a
hand-built overlay is not a few lines of `pefile`, or, failing that, an audit finding that lies in shared or hashed
features, where an overlay cannot reach.

### Reviewer C (generalist systems security)

**Summary.** Two kinds of malware detector, feature-based and raw-byte, cannot have their explanations compared, because
one explains named features and the other unnamed bytes. The paper records during feature extraction which bytes feed
which feature, and uses this to move explanations both ways. Two defender tasks follow: comparing a GBDT and MalConv
file by file (shared bytes, a prediction of attack carry-over, disagreement labels), and auditing MalConv over a corpus
for header fields an attacker can change, with a test-time countermeasure.

**Strengths.**
1. A concrete, defender-side use for explanations, with real attacks rather than only deletion
   (`_f_applications.tex:76-137, 272-301`).
2. The countermeasure is reported with its cost and its side effect on other fields (`_f_applications.tex:287-299`).
3. Clear plain-language motivation (`_b_introduction.tex:9-14`).

**Weaknesses (most severe first).**
1. **The "prediction before any edit" in 6.1 is beaten by simply running the edit.** The defender "can compute the
   attacker's choice of fields itself" (`_f_applications.tex:122-124`) and knows the edit rules (scramble ignored
   fields, draw valid values from benign files). It can therefore apply the edits and re-score the GBDT, at one
   extraction per file, the same cost as the pullback, and observe the drop exactly instead of predicting it at
   Spearman 0.48. The paper does not say why a defender would predict rather than simulate. Unless there is an answer
   (e.g., the attacker's values are unknown and a single simulated draw predicts the realized drop poorly), Table 2
   measures something a defender does not need. *Verified that the paper does not address it. Inferred that one
   simulated draw would predict well; resolve by rerunning the edits with a second seed of benign values and
   correlating the two drops.*
2. **What task is newly possible?** For PE, each application's findings could be produced by hand mapping (the paper
   admits this for 6.1, `_f_applications.tex:43-45`). The contribution is automation and byte resolution at one
   extraction. That is a real but incremental gain, and it is only as convincing as the generality, which rests on
   one extractor.
3. **Disagreement triage stops at labels.** The paper says each label "points the defender to a different response"
   (`_f_applications.tex:181-186`), but neither response is tried: no new feature is added for a "blind" gap, and no
   analyst inspects "oppose" bytes. 25 files per cell, top-1% and top-5% cutoffs chosen without justification.
4. **The audit's mitigation lacks an adaptive attacker.** After zeroing, the obvious attacker moves to the DOS stub
   and slack (Reviewer B, W2) or to unaudited fields. The "other fields" column (8.2%) is a weak stand-in for
   adaptation.
5. **Body length.** The body runs to page 15 against a 12-page limit (settled as "not yet", noted only because the
   cuts will decide which of the above can be answered in the main text).

**Questions.** (1) Why predict the GBDT's drop rather than simulate the edit? (2) What would a defender do differently
after triage, and did it help? (3) What happens to the 81% attack under your countermeasure?

**Score: Weak reject** (confidence 3/4).

**One change to move up a level:** answer W1 with data, or reframe Table 2 as validating the shared-reliance map
(the pullback's byte-level evidence is calibrated to what real edits do) rather than as a defender tool.

### Meta-review

- **Agreement.** All three see a clean method with honest positioning against G-DeepSHAP, a careful evaluation
  protocol, and real (if modest) security experiments. All three question whether the applications show something a
  PE expert could not obtain with a format parser. A and B both note that the pushforward's distinctive mechanism
  (splitting shared bytes) is not shown to matter.
- **Disagreement.** A values the problem-space/feature-space formalization and is lenient on the applications. B and
  C judge the paper by its security payoff and find it narrow: header reliance is known, the 6.1 prediction has a
  cheaper exact alternative, and the mitigation does not cover the strongest known attack.
- **Likely outcome: Reject** (1 Weak accept, 2 Weak reject). CCS 2026 has no major-revision outcome (Accept / Minor
  revision / Reject), and **a paper rejected in Cycle A may not be resubmitted to Cycle B**. A Cycle-A reject
  therefore costs the whole CCS year. The discussion would turn on B-W1 and C-W1. If both are answered, a minor
  revision becomes plausible.

## 3. Threat models (brief)

- **Shared setting** (`_f_applications.tex:18-29`): adequate. Loader-ignored bytes and valid field values follow
  Demetrio et al.; no appending or packing is stated as out of scope with a reason; extractor/loader parse
  differentials are explicitly excluded.
- **6.1 attacker** "explains only MalConv" (`_f_applications.tex:77-79`): looks shaped to the result. Against a
  deployed ensemble, neither model is white-box. The defender-red-teaming reading is defensible; say plainly that the
  defender chooses this attacker to ask how far an attack on one member carries over, not that attackers are like
  this.
- **6.2 threat model** (`_f_applications.tex:209-211`): consistent with the experiment (the attacker needs no model
  access, only knowledge of the reliance). But the paper's own 6.1 result (81% via DOS stub and slack) is a stronger
  attack within the same setting, and 6.2 does not mention it. The mitigation is credible only for the attack it
  measures. A stronger attacker against the zeroed MalConv is settled as deferred (it goes with retraining), and it is
  not fatal **provided 6.2 says what the mitigation covers.** Scope it explicitly to "reliance on content the file
  already has". Name the DOS-stub and slack surface from 6.1 (81%) in 6.2 as a separate, known problem with a known
  fix (not reading the DOS header; Demetrio et al. 2019) that zeroing the audited fields does not address. Today this
  admission sits only in the discussion (`_h_discussion.tex:44-47`), and a reviewer who connects 6.1's 81% with 6.2's
  16.8% on their own will read its absence from 6.2 as concealment.

## 5. Novelty and positioning

- **Searches** (web, 2026-10-21): attribution transfer through non-differentiable preprocessing; byte-level
  localization of EMBER explanations; global/structural explanations of raw-byte detectors; explanation transfer
  across representations. **Nothing found that maps feature attributions of an engineered extractor to bytes, or
  pushes byte attributions into such features.** The gap stated in `docs/related_work.md` (items 2, 3, 6) still holds.
- **Not covered by `docs/related_work.md` or the paper:**
  - Laigle, Chesneau, Salmon, "From black box to clear insights: explainable AI in malware detection with MalConv2"
    (SSTIC 2025): local explanations of MalConv2 at the level of *function offsets*, for PE and ELF. Another instance
    of "summarize raw-byte saliency over parsed structures." Low risk; one clause in §2.4 next to Aryal et al.
  - Hwang et al., "SHAP-based explanations are sensitive to feature representation" (FAccT 2025) is in
    `docs/related_work.md` but not cited in the paper. It supports the claim that the extractor shapes the
    explanation. Optional.
- **Closest prior work and the stated difference:**
  - *G-DeepSHAP:* the difference (a stage that is a program, with P obtained by instrumentation; plus the reverse
    direction) is stated clearly and fairly (`_b2_background.tex:105-111, 125-131`). Good.
  - *Region overlays (Coull & Gardner; Bose et al.; Aryal et al.):* the stated difference is "splits the credit of
    shared bytes among the named features automatically, for any extractor" (`_b2_background.tex:96-99,
    134-137`). The paper's own audit result does not exercise the "shared bytes" part (B-W1), and "any extractor" is
    shown for one (B-W3). **This is the positioning a reviewer will attack.**
  - *Virtual inspection layers:* fairly described.

### 9.1 Abstract (replaces `_a_abstract.tex:3-18`)

> Static malware detectors come in two kinds: models over engineered features, such as gradient-boosted trees on
> EMBER features, and deep networks that read a file's raw bytes. Their explanations live in different spaces. A
> feature-based detector's explanation names features without saying where in the file their evidence lies; a
> raw-byte detector's explanation marks bytes that have no name and mean something different in every file. We
> observe that every engineered feature is computed from specific bytes, and record this, by instrumenting the feature
> extractor once, as a \emph{provenance matrix}: each byte's share of each feature, which for fields, counts, and
> hashed counts is its Shapley value. This one matrix transfers explanations in both directions. The \emph{pullback}
> places feature attributions on the bytes, exactly for linear models over additive features; the \emph{pushforward}
> places byte attributions in named features and comes close to the pullback's exact inverse in one step. On
> EMBER-2024 with a gradient-boosted tree and MalConv, the pullback is as faithful as window occlusion at about a
> hundredth of its time, and at byte resolution removes more evidence than any direct explanation we tested, from one
> feature extraction instead of tens of thousands. Comparing the two detectors file by file, it shows that the bytes
> both rely on are worth more to each than its own, and predicts which of the tree's detections an attacker guided
> only by MalConv also weakens. Pushed forward, MalConv's per-file explanations become named reliance that recurs
> across files, about twice as faithful as a surrogate model's: changing the confirmed header fields only as the
> Windows loader allows evades 12.4\% of detected held-out malware, twice the rate of other loader-ignored fields, and
