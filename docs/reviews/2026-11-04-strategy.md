# Strategic review (2026-11-04): what the paper's strongest claim is, and what would make it hard to dismiss

Not a review of the draft's prose. The question: before the introduction fixes a direction, which piece of work moves
a skeptical program committee most? Inputs: Sections 3-6 as written, Table 7 with runs 122-123, `PLAN.md`,
`docs/status.md`, the R1-R16 register (`2026-11-02-body.md`), and a fresh literature search for 2025-26 work on the
same task (sources at the end). Recommendation in Section 5; the user decides.

## 1. The paper today, in one paragraph

Feature extractors that find pieces of the input, add up what each piece contributes, and apply a formula per feature
(sum-then-readout) can record those pieces while they run. From that record, one edit's effect on every feature is
computed exactly (Proposition 1, 0 wrong of 73,063 edits on EMBER), and a whole edit catalogue's effect is bounded as
the direction each feature can move (Theorem 2). The derived directions catch every error in a careful hand table (8
disagreements, each witnessed). Monotone detectors built on them keep 87-90% detection at 1% false positives (93.5%
unconstrained), no catalogue edit lowers their score, and they beat the expert set (92.2% vs 29.1% under additions)
and PV (whose hole the derivation finds before any attack runs; the adaptive search evades PV on 20 of 22).

**The strongest claim**, the one only this paper can make: *the constraints every constrained attack and robust
detector takes as input can be computed from the extractor, soundly, for the feature sets deployed detectors use.*
The detectors are the proof that this matters.

## 2. How a skeptical PC member summarizes it now

"They automate the per-feature labels of Incer et al.'s monotone construction for EMBER, catch a few hand-analysis
mistakes, and lose 4-6 points of detection. Robustness is by construction. The attacks are the secml-malware toolkit
at a small budget and the authors' own greedy search, which evades even the undefended model only 20% of the time; the
literature reports much stronger attacks on the same feature set. One extractor."

Each sentence is a fair reading of what is on the page. Three are fixable at the substance level, not by wording:

| Reading | Root cause | Fixable by |
|---|---|---|
| "lose 4-6 points; 1,060 features dropped" | the derivation records only which way a feature moves; everything two-sided is dropped | W1/W3 below |
| "attacks are weak" | small-budget toolkit; a greedy search handicapped to the toolkit's query count | W2 below |
| "they automate Incer's labels" | the paper's application is the oldest consumer of directions, and the newest one (ERDALT) is not discussed | literature, Section 3 |
| "one extractor" | R4 | late, interchangeable (user's settled decision); not considered further here |

## 3. Literature since our last pass

**Gimenez et al.'s main method is ERDALT, which our paper does not mention** (arXiv 2508.10038, read in full from
`papers/`). Their Proposition 2: every robust detector decomposes into a post-processing function g and a monotone
classifier. ERDALT learns g as a linear layer, trained so that every *perturbation vector* (feature difference between
an attacked and an original file) seen in attacks maps to a nonnegative vector; their example is an API substitution
(CreateFile -> CreateFileEx), where neither count is monotone but their sum is. Proposition 3: if the set of
perturbation vectors is known during training (and finite, independent of the file), ERDALT is robust. They call it
"empirically robust" because the set is learned from attacks. Their Table 2: ERDALT (neural network) 93.0% AUC, 96.0%
robust; PV + random forest 95.2% / 100%; adversarial training 97.6% / ~95%.

What this means for us:
- **§6 under-portrays Gimenez et al.** It describes their necessity result and PV, not ERDALT, which is their
  headline framework. Fix in §6 ¶3 (accuracy of cited work outranks a sharper argument).
- **It sharpens our story.** Proposition 3 needs exactly what our framework computes: the set of feature changes the
  threat model allows, known in advance rather than sampled. Theorem 2 computes its sign envelope; a coupling-aware
  version would compute the linear structure ERDALT learns. Our sums re-encoding (Section 4.3) is already a hand-chosen
  g of this kind (it recovers about two points by making normalized readouts one-sided).
- **It is a missing baseline.** ERDALT learns from attacks, so it should have PV's hole. One Table 7 row (their code is
  public); a neural network, which Table 7 otherwise does not use.

**Zainab et al., "Breaking Windows Malware Detection" (arXiv 2609.34456, Sep 2026; Dyrmishi, Bostani, Cavallaro,
Cordy).** Nine problem-space attacks against eight detectors, including an EMBER-2024 GBDT. Mean evasion on EMBER-24:
MAB-Malware 83.4%, MiniMal 30.6%, GAMMA shift 12.7%. MAB-Malware and GAMMA shift together cover about 99% of the
adversarial examples the other attacks produce. Adversarial hardening is "highly attack- and model-dependent" and can
increase susceptibility to unseen attacks. Their setup (budgets, dataset) differs from ours; numbers not comparable
directly. What it means:
- **Our toolkit is not the strongest available attack on this feature set.** A reviewer who knows this paper (same
  community as Dyrmishi et al., whom we cite) will ask why MAB-Malware is absent. We deferred it because "the full
  search is the stronger evidence"; that rationale no longer holds once the full search reaches only 20%.
- **It supports our motivation.** Their hardening finding is the empirical form of our argument that a defense built
  from attacks protects only against those attacks.
- MAB-Malware's actions are mostly in our catalogue (overlay append, add/append section, rename, remove debug and
  certificate, break checksum; code randomization is not). It is therefore a strong *in-catalogue* attacker for the
  monotone rows, and a strong held-out attacker for PV and adversarial training.

**Giri et al., "Towards Certified Malware Detection" (arXiv 2604.20495, Apr 2026).** Randomized smoothing with feature
ablation; certificates within a feature-space radius. Not the same task; one clause in related work at most. Like
Gimenez et al., it shows that the community wants certificates for malware, but its radius says nothing about which
edits stay inside it.

**Chen et al. 2020 (re-checked).** Verified training with symbolic intervals over Hidost features, 92% verified robust
accuracy for bounded subtree insertion. Their bounds are magnitudes (how many subtrees), but over a feature set where
an edit's reach is readable from feature names.

**Novelty verdict: unchanged.** No 2025-26 work computes feature-space constraints, side effects, or directions from
the extractor. The closest are still learned (Bostani et al. 2025, ERDALT) or hand-written (Simonetto, Dyrmishi). The
new works raise the bar on two other fronts: the attacks we must withstand, and the robust-by-design construction we
must compare with.

## 4. The three weaknesses, re-judged

### W1/W3: the robustness costs detection, because the derivation keeps only signs

The monotone detectors drop or neutralize 1,060 of 2,480 features because some catalogue operation moves them both
ways. Two different reasons can make a feature two-sided, and they need different fixes:
- **Coupled movement** (rename moves one section from hash bucket i to bucket j; overwriting slack moves byte counts
  between byte values with the total fixed). Each coordinate moves both ways, but a combination is fixed or
  one-sided. This is ERDALT's case, and C2 (coupling) in our original model, which the paper dropped. The fix is a
  coupling-aware derivation that computes such combinations (a computed g), then trains the monotone model on them.
- **Truly two-sided movement** (a header field an edit can set to anything). No re-encoding helps. Only a narrower
  threat model (drop the operation) or a magnitude bound under a budget can keep the feature.

**Magnitude bounds: deprioritize.** My earlier lean toward magnitude bounds does not survive the literature. Gimenez et
al. argue directly that magnitude-bounded certificates do not fit malware ("the magnitude of perturbation itself is
insignificant"). Our own A' found certified budgets never above 4 KB. Chen et al.'s bounds work on Hidost because an
edit's reach there is small and readable from feature names; EMBER's whole-file statistics are not like that.

**What we do not know yet, and can find out cheaply:** how the 1,060 split between coupled and truly two-sided
movement, and which operations cause them. The derived tables already hold this per operation. If most are coupled
(rename, slack, the DOS stub), a computed g is a strong framework extension and the natural reply to ERDALT. If most
are truly two-sided under one or two operations, the honest and useful result is a **price list**: how much detection
each allowed operation costs a provably robust detector. A defender can read it, and only a derivation can produce it
without running every attack against every candidate model. Either outcome gives a Table 7 story that is more than
"automated labels."

### W2: the attack side is weaker than the framework allows, and weaker than the literature

- The adaptive search is greedy, draws about 25 random candidates per step, stops at the first step where no candidate
  improves, and is capped at 100-200 scored candidates to match the toolkit's query count. The diagnosis of run
  108 vs 119 shows most of its misses are unlucky first-step draws, not exhausted budgets
  (`docs/direction-A-A1-design.md`).
- Matching query counts is fair between black-box attacks. It hides the framework's one attack-side advantage:
  scoring a candidate costs a prediction, not an extraction. A search that scores thousands of candidates per file
  (with restarts or a beam instead of a strict-improvement stop) costs seconds per file and is still white-box
  honest, since every kept step is written to disk and re-extracted.
- Why it matters beyond the attack column: Table 7's baselines (PV, adversarial training, unconstrained) are judged
  by the attacks we run. Weak attacks flatter them and blunt the contrast with the monotone rows, whose guarantee does
  not depend on attack strength. The "still detected after every attack" column becomes the paper's strongest
  empirical evidence only if the attacks are strong.
- The user worried about attack strength already (session 10); runs 124-125 raise the toolkit budget, which helps,
  but neither adds MAB-Malware nor lifts the adaptive search's handicap.

## 5. Recommendation

Do not write the introduction yet. Do three things, in this order, each with its go/no-go:

1. **Diagnostic: why are the 1,060 features two-sided?** (hours; existing derived tables; under ten minutes to run.)
   Per feature group and per operation: coupled (a fixed or one-sided combination exists among the sums the
   operation moves) or truly two-sided. Hypothesis: most are coupled, through rename, slack overwrite, and the DOS
   stub. **Decides between a computed re-encoding (the ERDALT reply) and a price list.** Then mock its Table 7 rows
   before any code, per the usual rule.
2. **Strong attacks.** (a) The adaptive search with a realistic scoring budget (thousands of predicted candidates
   per file, no strict-improvement stop), still re-extracting every kept step. Go/no-go: it beats the eight-attack
   toolkit union on the undefended model (55%); if it does not, the framework's attack-side claim stays out of the
   paper. (b) MAB-Malware as a held-out attack against every Table 7 row. Hypothesis: it evades PV and adversarial
   training often and the monotone rows never on files in the population. Both are runs over ten minutes, so
   smoke-test, then `run/126`+.
3. **Small fixes now:** §6 ¶3 describes ERDALT in its authors' terms; Zainab et al. join the toolkit sentence in §6
   ¶1 or the evaluation's attack setup.

After 1-2: decide whether ERDALT becomes a Table 7 row (likely yes if 1 points to a computed re-encoding, since that
is the head-to-head), and only then write the introduction around whichever result is strongest.

**A possible sharper headline, for the user to judge** (it changes what the application claims; not adopted):
*Gimenez et al. proved that every robust detector is a monotone classifier over some feature mapping, and obtain the
mapping by hand or by learning it from attacks. We compute it from the extractor.* This fits our related work, puts the
detector result on a cited theorem, and makes ERDALT and PV the natural baselines. It depends on item 1.

**Not now:** magnitude bounds (Section 4); a second instance (user's settled decision); the scale-up (mechanical, the
user's call once the rows are fixed); the introduction, background, and discussion.
