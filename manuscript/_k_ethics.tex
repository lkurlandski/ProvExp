\paragraph{Malware.} We use the PE files of EMBER-2024~\cite{joyce2025ember}, a public research dataset of benign and
malicious executables. We never execute them: every experiment parses the files, computes features, and runs
classifiers on the bytes. Perturbed and triggered files are written only to storage with restricted access, never
executed, and not released; our artifacts contain features, provenance matrices, explanations, and model scores, not
binaries. % TODO: confirm the access terms under which the binaries were obtained.

\paragraph{Evasion findings.} The audit identifies header fields whose loader-safe modification evades a released research
model, MalConv as distributed with EMBER-2024. Which changes the loader allows follows the format's documentation; the
modified files are not executed. We do not evaluate any
deployed commercial detector. Reliance of MalConv on header content is already documented and publicly
exploited~\cite{demetrio2019explaining}; our contribution is the method that finds such reliance automatically, which
we intend for defenders auditing their own detectors before deployment, together with the mitigation the audit points
to. We judge that publishing the method and the fields benefits defenders more than attackers, since an attacker who
can query a detector can already search the header for evasive changes directly.

\paragraph{Poisoned models.} The planted-trigger experiments train backdoored models on a dataset we control, solely as
a ground truth for evaluating explanations. The triggers are documented in the paper, the poisoned models are not
deployed, and the poisoning technique (a fixed pattern with relabeled training samples) is standard in the literature.

\paragraph{Human subjects.} None. The work involves no human participants and no personal data.
